# The Chosen Word — Audience and Setting Patterns

**Last updated:** 2026-10-21
**Status:** Draft — Phase 1, Loop 2

Observations are labeled **[OBS]**. Interpretations are labeled **[INTERP]** and are tentative unless marked otherwise.

## Audience Categories

Six distinct audience/setting types are present in the catalog:

| Category | Description | Entry Count (approx.) |

## Pattern 1 — Against Temptation: Deuteronomy, Exclusively

**[OBS]** Every response Christ gives to Satan's three temptations comes from Deuteronomy. Not Isaiah, not Psalms, not the commandments of Exodus. Deuteronomy alone.

- Deut 8:3 — "Man shall not live by bread alone" (response to physical need/provision)
- Deut 6:16 — "Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God" (response to a challenge to God's reliability)
- Deut 6:13 — "Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only" (response to an offer of worldly power)

**[INTERP]** Deuteronomy contains Moses's farewell covenant addresses — Israel's most comprehensive and direct statement of where ultimate loyalty belongs. All three passages Christ selects address the fundamental question of who governs a person's life: God or something else. Whether this reflects Christ's particular regard for Deuteronomy as the covenant foundation, or simply that these were the most direct texts available for the specific temptations presented, is an open question for future study. Tentative.

## Pattern 2 — Against Hostile Questioners: Meeting Them in Their Own Texts

**[OBS]** When challenged by Pharisees, scribes, Sadducees, or lawyers, Christ draws from the widest variety of OT sources in the catalog — Hosea, 1 Samuel, Numbers, Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Deuteronomy, Isaiah, Psalms, Jonah.

**[OBS]** A recurring rhetorical move: Christ answers hostile questions by citing a text from within the questioners' own recognized canon — often a text they knew but had misread or ignored.

Examples:
- Sabbath grain dispute → David ate temple bread too (1 Sam 21). "Have ye not read?"
- Sabbath grain dispute → Priests work in the temple on the Sabbath (Num 28). "Have ye not read?"
- Tradition dispute → Isaiah already condemned this pattern (Isa 29:13). "Well did Esaias prophesy of you."
- Resurrection denial → Moses himself called God the God of the living at the burning bush (Exod 3:6). "Have ye not read?"
- Greatest commandment → The answer is already in your law (Deut 6:5 + Lev 19:18).

**[OBS]** The citation formula "have ye not read?" appears exclusively in hostile contexts. It implies the answer was already accessible; the problem is not ignorance of the text but failure to read it correctly.

**[INTERP]** Christ does not argue from new authority against hostile questioners — he argues that they have misunderstood what their own scriptures already say. This is a consistent pattern, not an occasional tactic. Whether this reflects a deliberate pedagogical strategy or simply the nature of legal/interpretive disputes is a question worth examining in official Church sources. Tentative.

## Pattern 3 — Public Teaching: The Known Law + Isaiah as Mission Statement

**[OBS]** In public settings (crowds, synagogues), Christ draws primarily from the Pentateuch for moral teaching and from Isaiah when explaining his own identity or mission.

The most striking public Isaiah citation: Luke 4:18–19 (Q-052). Christ reads Isaiah 61:1–2 aloud from a synagogue scroll and declares "This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears." This is the only moment in the catalog where Christ reads OT text publicly from a scroll and directly claims its fulfillment.

**[OBS]** The Sermon on the Mount (and its Bountiful parallel) is the largest single concentration of public OT citation. All sources are from the Pentateuch — the recognized law. No Psalms, no Isaiah, no Daniel in the antitheses structure.

**[INTERP]** For a general audience, Christ appears to anchor in the law texts everyone knows before elevating to a higher standard. The Pentateuch provides shared ground; the antithetical structure ("ye have heard ... but I say") requires that shared ground. Tentative.

## Pattern 4 — Private Disciples: The Prophetic and Eschatological Texts

**[OBS]** With the inner circle alone, two OT sources appear that do not appear in public or hostile contexts:

- **Daniel** — Olivet Discourse only. Both Daniel citations (Dan 9:27/11:31 and Dan 7:13) appear exclusively in private teaching to the Twelve.
- **Zechariah** — Last Supper only. "I will smite the shepherd, and the sheep shall be scattered" (Zech 13:7) is spoken to the Eleven on the way to Gethsemane.

**[OBS]** Isaiah also appears in private settings: Isa 6:9–10 (explaining parables to disciples) and Isa 66:24 (stumbling block teaching to disciples). But Isaiah appears in other settings too. Daniel and Zechariah are private-only.

**[INTERP]** Daniel's content — the abomination of desolation, the Son of Man coming in clouds — may have been considered too eschatologically charged or too prone to political misreading for public audiences. Christ appears to share it only with those who can receive it within the proper interpretive frame. Zechariah 13:7 is deeply personal (it foretells the disciples' own scattering) and may not have been appropriate for public declaration. These are interpretations requiring further study. Tentative.

## Pattern 5 — The Passion Arc: From Law to Psalms

**[OBS]** As Christ moves through the final week, the OT books he cites shift dramatically. The Pentateuch law texts (Exodus, Deuteronomy, Leviticus) that dominate the Sermon and many disputes are almost entirely absent from the Passion narrative. Psalms and Isaiah take over.

**Passion week OT citations by book:**
- Temple cleansing: Isaiah (+ Jeremiah as composite)
- Temple courts: Psalms (Ps 8, Ps 118 — three citations)
- Last Supper: Zechariah, Isaiah, Psalms
- Trial: Daniel, Psalms
- Cross: Psalms only

**[OBS]** The two words Christ speaks from the cross are both Psalm citations:
- "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" (Ps 22:1) — Matthew's account
- "Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit" (Ps 31:5) — Luke's account

These are the final OT words in Christ's recorded speech. Both are from the Psalms. Both are addressed to the Father personally, not to any human audience.

**[INTERP]** The Psalms function as Christ's most intimate scriptural voice — the language he uses when speaking from his own experience rather than teaching, rebuking, or answering. This is tentative but the pattern across the catalog is consistent: Psalms cluster around the moments of greatest personal intensity (betrayal, trial, cross). The law texts cluster around teaching and dispute. Whether this reflects something about how Christ understood the Psalms — as a kind of inspired personal prayer literature — is a question for future loops with official Church sources. Tentative.

## Pattern 6 — The Nephite Shift: Isaiah Expands Massively

**[OBS]** The 3 Nephi teaching contains the same OT-book structure as the Galilean sermon for the antitheses section (Pentateuch law), then shifts dramatically for the extended gathering discourse.

In 3 Ne 20–22, Isaiah alone accounts for the overwhelming majority of citations — Isaiah 52 is quoted in five separate entries, with some verses repeated. Isaiah 54 is quoted in its entirety. No other OT prophet comes close.

**[OBS]** In the Gospels, Christ quotes Isaiah in roughly 6–7 distinct citation events across his entire ministry. In 3 Nephi's extended discourse alone, Isaiah appears in at least 8 distinct passages. Christ also commands the Nephites to "search these things diligently" (regarding Isaiah) in 3 Ne 23:1.

**[OBS]** The Malachi chapters (3 Ne 24–25) are unique in the catalog: Christ explicitly explains that he is delivering them because the Nephites did not have this record. This is the only instance where Christ states a reason for quoting a particular OT text at length.

**[INTERP]** The 3 Nephi discourse's Isaiah saturation may reflect the covenant context of the Nephite gathering — Isaiah's prophecies of Israel's latter-day gathering and restoration are directly applicable to Christ's Nephite audience in a way they are not to his Galilean audience. The audience determines which OT prophetic content is most relevant. Tentative.

## Pattern 7 — Hosea as a Pharisee-Specific Diagnostic

**[OBS]** Hosea 6:6 appears twice in the catalog — both times in Matthew, both times in disputes involving Pharisees and Sabbath/purity issues. No other Gospel records it. No other audience hears it.

- Matt 9:13: "Go ye and learn what that meaneth, I will have mercy, and not sacrifice" — said to Pharisees questioning why he eats with sinners.
- Matt 12:7: "But if ye had known what this meaneth, I will have mercy, and not sacrifice, ye would not have condemned the guiltless" — said to Pharisees challenging Sabbath grain-picking.

**[OBS]** The framing differs: "go ye and learn" (first) vs. "if ye had known" (second). The first is a challenge to go study it; the second assumes they still haven't learned it after the first encounter.

**[INTERP]** This is the only case in the catalog where Christ appears to return to the same verse in two explicitly connected encounters with the same audience type. The progression suggests he regards Hos 6:6 as the diagnostic verse for the Pharisees' error — their misunderstanding of what God actually values. The shift in framing from "go learn" to "if ye had known" may indicate deliberate escalation. Tentative — requires checking whether the two Sabbath disputes are temporally close enough for that connection to be explicit. This is worth a focused future loop.

## Open Questions for Future Loops

2. Why does Daniel appear only with private disciples and at the trial? Are there official Church teachings that address this?
3. The Hosea 6:6 double citation warrants a focused loop: what is the temporal relationship between Matt 9 and Matt 12, and does the "go ye and learn" / "if ye had known" framing suggest deliberate escalation?
