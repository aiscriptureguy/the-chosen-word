## OT Original Context

Hosea is an eighth-century-BC prophet whose ministry was directed primarily at the northern kingdom of Israel (Ephraim) during a period of religious corruption and covenant unfaithfulness. The book is structured around the prophet's marriage to Gomer as a living metaphor of the Lord's relationship with faithless Israel.

[OBS] Hosea 6 begins with what appears to be Israel's voice — a call to repentance and return: "Come, and let us return unto the Lord: for he hath torn, and he will heal us; he hath smitten, and he will bind us up" (v. 1). Verses 1–3 speak with confidence about revival after two days, restoration on the third, and knowledge of the Lord that is as certain as the morning.

[OBS] The tone shifts abruptly in verse 4, when Jehovah speaks: "O Ephraim, what shall I do unto thee? O Judah, what shall I do unto thee? for your goodness is as a morning cloud, and as the early dew it goeth away." The opening repentance speech (vv. 1–3) is placed under a dark light — Israel's goodness is described as transient, evaporating by morning.

[OBS] Verse 5 escalates: "Therefore have I hewed them by the prophets; I have slain them by the words of my mouth: and thy judgments are as the light that goeth forth." The prophets' word has been the instrument of judgment on a people whose covenant faithfulness could not hold.

[OBS] Verse 6 — the cited verse — is the pivot: "For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice; and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings."

[OBS] The verse presents two contrasts: mercy over sacrifice, knowledge of God over burnt offerings. In the Hebrew, the word translated "mercy" is hesed — the covenant term for loyal, steadfast love, the love that maintains relationship and obligation. This is not generic kindness but the specific kind of love that covenant obligations require. [INTERP] The contrast is not between two religions (inner vs. outer) but between two expressions of covenant faithfulness: hesed (lived relational loyalty) and sacrifice (ritual performance). The prophet's diagnosis is that Israel has been maintaining the sacrificial cult while failing at hesed.

[OBS] Verse 7 follows immediately: "But they like men have transgressed the covenant: there have they dealt treacherously against me." The covenant-transgression language directly follows the hesed/sacrifice contrast, confirming that the failure in question is covenant infidelity rather than ritual negligence.

[OBS] Verses 8–11 describe specific acts of treachery — Gilead is bloodstained (v. 8); priests commit violence (v. 9); Israel is defiled (v. 10); Judah also faces a harvest of judgment (v. 11). The chapter ends without resolution.

[INTERP] Hosea 6:6 in its original setting is a prophetic diagnosis, not a liturgical instruction. It is not telling Israel to stop performing sacrifices in principle; it is diagnosing a condition in which sacrificial performance has continued while covenant loyalty (hesed) has collapsed. The implication is that sacrifice without hesed is not merely incomplete — it is the wrong category entirely. The Lord did not ask for sacrifice as the primary expression of covenant relationship; hesed is what he wanted.

[INTERP] The phrase "knowledge of God" parallels hesed in the verse structure: mercy // knowledge of God; sacrifice // burnt offerings. The knowledge of God in Hosea is not intellectual but relational and behavioral — it means living within the covenant relationship as it was intended. Its absence is covenant treachery (v. 7). This is different from how "knowledge" functions in Greek or later philosophical frameworks.

[OBS] Hosea 6:6 is unique in the OT prophets in stating the contrast this starkly and this directly. Other prophets make similar critiques (Amos 5:21–24; Isaiah 1:11–17; Micah 6:6–8) but Hosea 6:6 is the formulation Christ twice quotes verbatim.

## NT Citation Occasions

### Occasion 1 — Matt 9:13: Sinners at Table

- Setting and audience: A house meal (probably Matthew's house, immediately after his call). Publicans and sinners are eating with Jesus and his disciples. The Pharisees observe this and challenge the disciples: "Why eateth your Master with publicans and sinners?" (v. 11). Christ hears the challenge and responds directly.
- How Christ introduces the citation: "But go ye and learn what that meaneth, I will have mercy, and not sacrifice: for I am not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance" (v. 13).
- Citation type: Direct quotation (partial — the first half of Hos 6:6 only: "I will have mercy, and not sacrifice").
- Use: Reproof — directed at the Pharisees who object to his table companions
- [OBS] The phrase "go ye and learn what that meaneth" is a rabbinic formula for directing a questioner to study a text rather than receiving instruction passively. Christ is not explaining the text — he is telling them to go find out what it means.
- [OBS] Christ cites only the first clause of Hos 6:6 here: "I will have mercy, and not sacrifice." He does not cite "the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings." He then appends his own interpretive statement: "for I am not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance."
- [INTERP] The "go ye and learn" formula implies that the Pharisees already have this text in their canon and have not yet understood it. The rebuke is not that they are ignorant of Hosea but that they have the text and have missed its meaning.
- [INTERP] Christ's application of hesed to table-fellowship with sinners is a specific concretization of the principle. Hosea used hesed as covenant loyalty; Christ applies it to social boundary-crossing — eating with those the purity system excluded. He is arguing that inclusion of sinners is the form hesed takes in his ministry. This is an interpretive move, clearly labeled.
- [OBS] The occasion is created by Matthew's call (v. 9) and the shared meal. The Pharisees' objection is about social-ritual purity (eating with sinners), not about temple sacrifice directly. Christ's citation of a text about sacrifice vs. hesed moves the argument from purity law to a broader prophetic principle.

### Occasion 2 — Matt 12:7: Sabbath Grain-Picking

- Setting and audience: Jesus and disciples walk through grain fields on the sabbath; disciples pluck and eat grain. The Pharisees challenge Christ directly: "Behold, thy disciples do that which is not lawful to do upon the sabbath day" (v. 2). Christ responds with a series of legal arguments before citing Hosea.
- How Christ introduces the citation: "But if ye had known what this meaneth, I will have mercy, and not sacrifice, ye would not have condemned the guiltless" (v. 7).
- Citation type: Direct quotation (same partial form as Occasion 1 — first clause only).
- Use: Reproof — escalated in framing from Occasion 1
- [OBS] In this occasion Christ gives Hosea 6:6 as the third and culminating argument in a sequence: (1) David and the showbread (vv. 3–4), (2) priests who "profane" the sabbath in the temple and are blameless (v. 5), (3) "in this place is one greater than the temple" (v. 6), (4) Hosea 6:6 (v. 7), (5) "For the Son of man is Lord even of the sabbath day" (v. 8). The citation appears within a legal argument structure, not as a standalone statement.
- [OBS] The framing has changed from Occasion 1. In Matt 9:13 Christ says "go ye and learn what that meaneth." In Matt 12:7 he says "if ye had known what this meaneth." The shift from imperative to conditional — from "go learn" to "if you had known" — implies a judgment: they should have known by now.
- [INTERP] This is the escalation pattern noted in Loop 2's audience-patterns analysis. The two occasions of Hos 6:6 in Matthew are not simply repetitions. The framing shifts from invitation-to-study to judgment-for-not-having-studied. Whether this represents a deliberate rhetorical escalation by Christ, or is a function of different rhetorical contexts that happened to produce different framings, cannot be determined from the text alone. Labeled [INTERP] and marked as a question for Chase.
- [OBS] The Sabbath grain-picking occasion is different in kind from the sinners-at-table occasion. Sinners-at-table is a purity and social boundary dispute; Sabbath grain-picking is a legal sabbath observance dispute. Christ applies the same Hosea text to two different categories of Pharisaic objection — both of which involve the Pharisees treating law-performance as the primary criterion and overlooking mercy.
- [INTERP] The argument culminates in "the Son of man is Lord even of the sabbath day" (v. 8). This means the Hosea citation is not Christ's final word — it is preparation for a self-identification claim. The hesed/sacrifice principle clears the ground; the "Lord of the sabbath" claim plants the flag.

## Across-Occasion Analysis

[OBS] Both citations use only the first clause of Hos 6:6: "I will have mercy, and not sacrifice." Christ does not quote the second half ("the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings") in either occasion.

[OBS] Both occasions involve the Pharisees as the challenged party. This is the only repeated OT passage that appears exclusively in Pharisee-dispute contexts within Matthew.

[OBS] Both occasions involve Christ defending conduct that the Pharisees consider a violation of religious law — table purity in one case, sabbath observance in the other. In both cases, the Pharisees' objection is framed as a legal-observance concern, and Christ's citation of Hosea re-frames the issue as a hesed-priority concern.

[OBS] The framing changes:
- Matt 9:13: "go ye and learn what that meaneth" — a directive
- Matt 12:7: "if ye had known what this meaneth" — a conditional judgment

[INTERP] The cumulative effect of citing Hosea 6:6 twice in different disputes, with a framing that escalates from "study this" to "you should have known," suggests that Christ was applying a specific prophetic diagnosis to a persistent pattern. The Pharisees' response to both occasions is the same structural error — privileging ritual correctness over the living quality that the ritual was meant to express. Whether Christ intended the citations as a deliberate teaching progression or whether the similarity is a function of a consistent pattern in his opponents is not stated in the text.

## What This Choice Reveals About Christ

[INTERP] Christ's use of Hosea 6:6 twice in Pharisee-dispute contexts suggests he found in this prophetic text an accurate diagnosis of the specific failure he was encountering. Hosea's critique of Israel — maintaining sacrifice while failing at hesed — is the same structural critique Christ makes of the Pharisees: maintaining legal observance while missing the covenant loyalty the law was meant to express.

[INTERP] The choice of Hosea rather than Amos, Isaiah, or Micah (all of whom make similar critiques) may indicate that Hosea 6:6's specific formulation — "I desired mercy, not sacrifice" — was the clearest statement of the diagnostic principle. Or it may indicate familiarity from other contexts. These remain matters of interpretation; the text does not say why this verse rather than another.

[INTERP] Both occasions involve boundary-crossing that the purity system excluded. Table-fellowship with sinners and disciples picking grain on the sabbath are both situations where the law's surface reading would condemn the action but where Christ argues the law's deeper intent does not. Hosea 6:6 provides the prophetic warrant for the distinction between law's surface and law's intent.

## Spiritual Principle

Occasion 1 (Matt 9:13, sinners at table): The form that mercy takes in Christ's ministry is inclusion — eating with the excluded. The principle from Hosea is applied to social boundary: hesed overrides purity-system exclusion.

Occasion 2 (Matt 12:7, Sabbath grain): The same principle applied to legal observance: sabbath law is not meant to condemn those whose need requires action. Hesed defines the sabbath's purpose; the sabbath does not override hesed.

Synthesis: Christ uses Hos 6:6 to press a consistent distinction: religious practice — sacrifice, purity law, sabbath observance — is not the primary covenant requirement. Hesed, covenant loyal love expressed in relational action, is. Religious practice is meaningful only when it expresses that love; when it conflicts with it, the text from the prophet says which takes priority.

## Interpretive Cautions

- "Mercy not sacrifice" does not mean sacrifices were wrong or useless. Both the Hosea text and Christ's citations are critiques of a prioritization failure, not abolitionist arguments. Do not present this as Christ condemning the temple system per se.
- The Hebrew hesed behind "mercy" is richer than the English word implies. It includes faithfulness, loyalty, and covenant obligation — not merely compassionate feeling. Any summary that collapses hesed to "being nice" misrepresents both the OT and the NT citation.
- The framing shift from "go learn" to "if you had known" is a textual observation. It may represent deliberate escalation, or it may reflect different rhetorical occasions. Avoid stating the escalation as an established fact rather than an observed pattern.
- Christ cites only half of Hos 6:6 in both occasions. The second half ("knowledge of God more than burnt offerings") is not cited. Do not import the full verse's implications into an analysis of Christ's use without noting that he stopped at the midpoint.
