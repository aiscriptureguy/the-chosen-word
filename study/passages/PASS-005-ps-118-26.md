## OT Original Context

[OBS] Psalm 118 is a thanksgiving psalm, the last of the Egyptian Hallel psalms (Ps 113–118) sung at Passover and other major festivals. Its opening and closing verses are identical: "O give thanks unto the Lord; for he is good: for his mercy endureth for ever" (vv. 1 and 29). This framing establishes the psalm as a song of enduring covenant faithfulness.

[OBS] The psalm moves through a narrative arc. The speaker begins by testifying of distress and divine deliverance: "I called upon the Lord in distress: the Lord answered me" (v. 5). He describes being surrounded by nations like bees but prevailing because the Lord was his help (vv. 10–13). Then comes a transition to the gates of the Lord and the entrance of the righteous (vv. 19–20).

[OBS] Verses 19–29, the section immediately surrounding v. 26, read as a processional or entrance liturgy — possibly associated with pilgrims arriving at the temple:

- v. 19: "Open to me the gates of righteousness: I will go into them, and I will praise the Lord"
- v. 20: "This gate of the Lord, into which the righteous shall enter"
- v. 21: "I will praise thee: for thou hast heard me, and art become my salvation"
- v. 22: "The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner"
- v. 23: "This is the Lord's doing; it is marvellous in our eyes"
- v. 24: "This is the day which the Lord hath made; we will rejoice and be glad in it"
- v. 25: "Save now, I beseech thee, O Lord: O Lord, I beseech thee, send now prosperity"
- v. 26: "Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the Lord: we have blessed you out of the house of the Lord"
- v. 27: "God is the Lord, which hath shewed us light: bind the sacrifice with cords, even unto the horns of the altar"
- v. 28: "Thou art my God, and I will praise thee: thou art my God, I will exalt thee"
- v. 29: "O give thanks unto the Lord; for he is good: for his mercy endureth for ever"

[OBS] Verse 25 contains "Save now, I beseech thee" — the Hebrew hosanna (hoshia-na), which becomes the crowd's cry in the Triumphal Entry accounts. This is one verse before the cited verse 26. The two are inseparable in the psalm's flow.

[OBS] Verse 26: "Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the Lord." In the psalm's liturgical setting, this appears to be a priestly blessing spoken to pilgrims arriving at the temple — an officiant speaking words of welcome to those entering the sanctuary. "We have blessed you out of the house of the Lord" in the second half of the verse confirms the speaker is inside (or on behalf of) the temple.

[OBS] Verse 22 — "The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner" — is itself one of the most-cited OT verses in the NT, and Christ quotes it in all three Synoptics (Matt 21:42; Mark 12:10; Luke 20:17). This verse sits within the same psalm, six verses before v. 26. Christ's use of Ps 118 is therefore broader than just v. 26 — he draws from multiple verses of this psalm.

[INTERP] In its liturgical setting, "Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the Lord" was a blessing for pilgrims — probably spoken at festival gatherings when crowds arrived at Jerusalem. It was not composed as a messianic prophecy in the narrow sense; it was a functional blessing for worshippers arriving at the temple. Its messianic application depends on identifying the "coming one" with the expected deliverer who will come in the Lord's name. This re-reading appears to have been current in Jewish expectation, as shown by the crowd's use of it at the Triumphal Entry.

[OBS] The crowds at the Triumphal Entry (Matt 21:9) shout: "Hosanna to the Son of David: Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord; Hosanna in the highest." The crowds are applying Ps 118:25–26 to Jesus as he enters Jerusalem — before Christ uses the verse himself. When Christ quotes it in Matt 23:39 and Luke 13:35, he is taking back a text that the crowds had applied to him, now turning it into a conditional or future statement.

## NT Citation Occasions

### Occasion 1 — Luke 13:35: Lament Over Jerusalem (Earlier Ministry)

- Setting and audience: The account in Luke 13:31–35 is placed earlier in Luke's narrative — while Jesus is still in Galilee or Perea, traveling toward Jerusalem. Pharisees warn him that Herod wants to kill him (v. 31). Christ responds with a reference to his ministry continuing for a set time before his death, then immediately extends into the Jerusalem lament.
- How Christ introduces the citation: "Behold, your house is left unto you desolate: and verily I say unto you, Ye shall not see me, until the time come when ye shall say, Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord" (v. 35).
- Citation type: Direct quotation of Ps 118:26 (partial — "Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord").
- Use: Fulfillment claim with conditional framing — the verse is placed as a future threshold
- [OBS] The lament in Luke 13:34: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, which killest the prophets, and stonest them that are sent unto thee; how often would I have gathered thy children together, as a hen doth gather her brood under her wings, and ye would not!" The grief expressed is prior rejection — "ye would not" (past tense). Christ has been trying to gather Jerusalem; Jerusalem has refused.
- [OBS] "Your house is left unto you desolate" (v. 35a) — the "house" is likely the temple. "Desolate" indicates abandonment, not physical destruction (though that may be implied). The statement is a judgment announcement.
- [OBS] The citation follows the judgment: "Ye shall not see me, until the time come when ye shall say, Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord." The "until" construction makes Ps 118:26 a condition or threshold: the desolation continues until the moment of recognition arrives.
- [INTERP] In Luke 13, the lament is spoken before the Triumphal Entry. This means Christ is using Ps 118:26 as a future threshold before the crowds have applied it to him. The sequence is: (1) Christ laments; (2) Christ pronounces judgment; (3) Christ names the condition under which he will be seen again — "when ye shall say" the psalm verse. Then in Luke 19, the crowds do say it at the entry.
- [INTERP] The emotional register is grief — "how often would I have gathered... and ye would not." The Ps 118:26 citation in this setting is not triumphant but sorrowful: it describes what is missing and what would need to happen for the relationship to be restored. It is a blessing that has been withheld.
- [INTERP] The phrase "the time come when ye shall say" is ambiguous. Does it refer to the Triumphal Entry (a few weeks away in narrative time)? Or to a future eschatological gathering when Israel will acknowledge Christ? Both readings have interpretive support. [Note: official Church sources would help clarify the LDS understanding of this "until." Flagged for verification.]

### Occasion 2 — Matt 23:39: Woes to Pharisees, Jerusalem Temple

- Setting and audience: The Jerusalem temple courts, last week of ministry — specifically at the end of the extended woe-discourse of Matthew 23. Christ has delivered seven woes against the scribes and Pharisees (vv. 13–36). He then addresses Jerusalem directly in a lament (vv. 37–39), the same content as Luke 13:34–35 but spoken here in a very different narrative position.
- How Christ introduces the citation: "For I say unto you, Ye shall not see me henceforth, till ye shall say, Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord" (v. 39).
- Citation type: Direct quotation of Ps 118:26 (same partial form as Luke 13:35).
- Use: Fulfillment claim / conditional departure — placed at the close of the woe-discourse
- [OBS] In Matthew's sequence, the seven woes precede this lament. The woes are severe: "hypocrites," "blind guides," "whited sepulchres," "generation of vipers." The lament in vv. 37–39 follows immediately, representing a different emotional register — grief rather than condemnation.
- [OBS] The lament in v. 37: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, thou that killest the prophets, and stonest them which are sent unto thee; how often would I have gathered thy children together, even as a hen gathereth her chickens under her wings, and ye would not!" This is nearly identical to Luke 13:34.
- [OBS] "Behold, your house is left unto you desolate" (v. 38). In Matthew's narrative, this follows Jesus's departure from the temple (24:1: "And Jesus went out, and departed from the temple"). The desolation announcement and the departure are continuous.
- [OBS] The citation: "Ye shall not see me henceforth, till ye shall say, Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord" (v. 39). The word "henceforth" in Matthew is more definitive than Luke's "until the time come" — it marks a clear departure moment. Christ is not traveling away from Jerusalem toward Jerusalem (as in Luke 13); he is departing the Jerusalem temple at the climax of his confrontation with its leaders.
- [INTERP] In Matthew, Ps 118:26 is placed at the moment of departure from the temple, after the most severe public denunciation of the religious leadership in the Gospel. The emotional register here contains both grief (the hen image) and finality ("henceforth"). The lament is not the beginning of a journey toward Jerusalem but the conclusion of Christ's temple ministry.
- [INTERP] The crowds have already used Ps 118:26 to welcome him (Matt 21:9 — Triumphal Entry, just days before). Christ now repeats it as a condition for his return. The verse that the crowds used as a welcome becomes the verse Christ uses to describe what recognition would actually require — and has not yet happened at the level of Jerusalem's leadership.

## Across-Occasion Analysis

[OBS] Both occasions use identical or nearly identical wording from Ps 118:26: "Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord." Neither adds the second half of the psalm verse ("we have blessed you out of the house of the Lord").

[OBS] Both occasions are paired with the same lament: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem... how often would I have gathered thy children... and ye would not." The lament content is nearly verbatim in both. The difference is context and sequence:

- Luke 13: The lament precedes arrival in Jerusalem. It is a prophecy spoken from outside. The tone is anticipatory grief.
- Matt 23: The lament concludes Christ's ministry in Jerusalem. It follows the woes. The tone is final grief with a note of definitive departure.

[INTERP] These two occasions of the same lament + Ps 118:26 citation may represent one saying given in two different settings (some scholars suggest Luke's is earlier and Matthew's is the final delivery at the temple), or they may be two different occasions that share a common emotional core. The speaker filter does not require resolving this — both are occasions where Christ speaks this citation, whatever the relationship between them. The emotional and rhetorical differences are real in both accounts as they stand.

[OBS] In Luke 13, the citation functions as a threshold not yet crossed — "until the time come when ye shall say." In Matt 23, the citation is more final — "henceforth... till ye shall say." Both uses are conditional, but the conditionality in Matthew has more of the quality of a departure announcement.

[INTERP] In both occasions, Christ uses a liturgical blessing (Ps 118:26 was used to welcome pilgrims at the temple) and converts it into a conditional statement about recognition. The psalm's original setting was a moment of joyful arrival; Christ's use is a statement about a recognition that has not yet happened and may not happen until a future time. This is a reversal of the psalm's emotional register — from welcome song to threshold not yet crossed.

[INTERP] The crowds at the Triumphal Entry did say "Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord" — and the crowd's use of the verse, sandwiched between these two Christ citations, provides a contrast. The crowd's use was present-tense welcome; Christ's citation in both laments is future-conditional. The crowd was citing the psalm in its original liturgical mode; Christ is pointing to something more and deeper that has not yet occurred.

## What This Choice Reveals About Christ

[INTERP] Christ's use of Ps 118:26 in two lament contexts reveals that he was engaged with the question of Jerusalem's recognition of him as something unfinished, even tragic. He is not celebrating the crowd's Hosannas; he is naming what genuine recognition would require — and what is missing.

[INTERP] The choice of a temple pilgrimage blessing ("Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the Lord") to describe what Jerusalem has not yet done is an inversion. The psalm was a blessing for those arriving at the house of God; Christ applies it to the conditions under which the relationship between Jerusalem and himself might be restored. The temple's own worship vocabulary is being used to diagnose the temple's failure.

[INTERP] The grief tone in both occasions is notable. This is the only repeated OT citation where the dominant emotional key is sorrow rather than argument, correction, or proclamation. Christ is not using Ps 118:26 to win a debate or establish a principle. He is using it to express what he wanted for Jerusalem — and what Jerusalem has not yet given him.

## Spiritual Principle

Occasion 1 (Luke 13:35, lament before Jerusalem): Recognition of Christ — genuine recognition, not a crowd's shout — is a threshold. The desolation of the house continues until that recognition comes. The psalm verse that meant welcome now means: until you truly mean it.

Occasion 2 (Matt 23:39, lament concluding temple ministry): The same principle stated at the point of departure. Jerusalem's leaders have heard everything and remained in the mode of opposition. The conditional "till ye shall say" implies that recognition is still possible — but it requires something that has not yet happened.

Synthesis: Christ uses Ps 118:26 to press toward authentic recognition — not the crowd's enthusiasm, not formal religious identity, but the specific acknowledgment that he comes in the Lord's name and is received as such. The psalm that said "welcome" at the gate becomes the threshold that measures the depth of a city's relationship with the One it has rejected.

## Interpretive Cautions

- The crowd at the Triumphal Entry does say "Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord" — which on the surface fulfills the condition. But Christ in Matt 23:39 speaks this condition again, after the entry. This suggests that the crowd's Hosanna was not the fulfillment Christ was pointing toward. Do not conflate the crowd's use with the fulfillment Christ describes.
- "Your house is left unto you desolate" is interpreted in some traditions as referring primarily to the destruction of the temple in AD 70, and in others as a spiritual abandonment that can be reversed. Do not present one interpretation as the only reading without noting the range.
- Whether Luke 13:35 and Matt 23:39 are two different occasions or the same saying delivered once and placed differently by the evangelists is a question in biblical scholarship. The passage records treat them as two distinct occasions (consistent with the speaker-filter methodology) but note that their relationship is uncertain.
- The "until" in both passages could refer to: (a) the Triumphal Entry (already past when Matthew records the saying); (b) a future eschatological gathering of Israel; (c) individual recognition at any point. The range of possibilities should be acknowledged, not collapsed.

## Questions for Chase

1. The crowd says "Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord" at the Triumphal Entry — but Christ uses the same verse twice in lament contexts as a future condition. Does the crowd's use feel to you like a genuine fulfillment, or more like a shadow of what Christ was looking for?
2. Is Christ grieving in these passages — or is he declaring judgment? Or both simultaneously? The hen image feels like grief; "your house is left unto you desolate" feels like a verdict. How do you hold those together?
3. Are there official Church sources that discuss what "until ye shall say, Blessed is he that cometh" refers to — the Triumphal Entry, or a future gathering of Israel, or something else? This distinction has real doctrinal weight and should be verified.
4. The emotional tone of Ps 118:26 in Christ's use is different from every other repeated citation in this study — it's the only one where grief is the dominant register. Does that stand out to you? What does it reveal about Christ's relationship with Jerusalem specifically?
5. The stone-rejected-cornerstone verse (Ps 118:22) is in the same psalm, and Christ also quotes that elsewhere. Would it be useful for the site to present Ps 118 as a psalm Christ draws from repeatedly — not just v. 26 — to give visitors a sense of how he engaged with a full text rather than isolated verses?
