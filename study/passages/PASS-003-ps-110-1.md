## OT Original Context

[OBS] Psalm 110 is a short psalm of seven verses, attributed to David in its superscription. It is addressed as a royal oracle — a divine declaration to a king or royal figure. The psalm is one of the most quoted OT texts in the NT; Psalm 110:1 alone is cited more frequently in the NT than any other single OT verse.

[OBS] Verse 1 reads: "The Lord said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool." The structure presents two distinct figures referred to as "Lord" — the first (Jehovah / the LORD in small capitals) speaks; the second (Adonai / "my Lord") is addressed. David, as the attributed speaker, calls the second figure "my Lord" — meaning someone David himself regards as his superior.

[OBS] Verse 2 continues: "The Lord shall send the rod of thy strength out of Zion: rule thou in the midst of thine enemies." The figure addressed in v. 1 is now described as ruling from Zion with divine authority.

[OBS] Verse 3 speaks of willing people "in the day of thy power, in the beauties of holiness from the womb of the morning." The language is royal and cosmic.

[OBS] Verse 4 introduces a second oracle: "The Lord hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek." This is one of only two explicit references to Melchizedek in the OT (the other is Genesis 14:18–20). The figure addressed in v. 1 is now identified as holding a priestly office — not the Levitical priesthood but an older, royal-priestly order named for Melchizedek, king of Salem.

[OBS] Verses 5–7 describe the Lord's action at the right hand of the king in a day of wrath — striking kings, judging nations, filling battlefields with dead, and ultimately lifting up the head.

[INTERP] In its OT setting, Psalm 110 is most naturally read as a royal enthronement psalm, celebrating or describing a Davidic king who receives divine authorization to sit in the position of honor at God's right hand, to rule over enemies, and to hold a priestly office in the Melchizedek order. The exact occasion for the psalm's composition is not stated. Some scholars suggest it was composed for a coronation; others connect it to a specific king. [Note: OT scholarship context falls outside approved sources; this is a general orientation, not a sourced claim.]

[INTERP] The theological weight of the psalm for later readers — including first-century Jews and early Christians — lay in the combination of royal authority (right-hand position, footstool of enemies) and priestly authority (Melchizedek order) in a single figure. No Levitical priest sat at God's right hand; no Davidic king held the Melchizedek priesthood by title. The psalm describes a figure who holds both.

[OBS] In the LDS doctrinal framework, Psalm 110:4 is significant because the Melchizedek Priesthood is foundational to the Restoration. The connection between Ps 110:1 (right-hand position) and Ps 110:4 (Melchizedek priest) gives the verse a resonance that extends beyond simple messianic identification. [Note: this connection warrants official Church source support before use in published content; flagged for verification.]

[OBS] The puzzle that makes Ps 110:1 theologically interesting is David's language: he calls this enthroned figure "my Lord." In the logic of the verse, a father does not call his son "my Lord." If the enthroned figure is merely a Davidic descendant in the biological sense, why would David use lordship language? This puzzle is the one Christ explicitly exploits in Matt 22.

## NT Citation Occasions

### Occasion 1 — Matt 22:41–45: Christ Poses a Question

- Setting and audience: Jerusalem temple courts, last week of ministry. After silencing the Sadducees (resurrection question) and the Pharisees (greatest commandment question), Christ takes the initiative and poses a question to the assembled Pharisees.
- How Christ introduces the citation: "What think ye of Christ? whose son is he? They say unto him, The Son of David. He saith unto them, How then doth David in spirit call him Lord, saying, The Lord said unto my Lord, Sit thou on my right hand, till I make thine enemies thy footstool? If David then call him Lord, how is he his son?" (vv. 41–45)
- Citation type: Direct quotation of Ps 110:1
- Use: Self-identification — Christ poses the riddle to expose the inadequacy of "Son of David" as a complete account of his identity
- [OBS] This is one of the rare places in the Synoptic Gospels where Christ himself initiates a doctrinal discussion rather than responding to a challenge. The Pharisees have just attempted to trap him with a question; he now poses a question they cannot answer.
- [OBS] The argument structure is: (1) Everyone agrees the Messiah is David's son; (2) David writes Ps 110 "in spirit" — meaning under divine inspiration; (3) David calls the Messiah "my Lord"; (4) a father does not call his biological descendant "my Lord"; (5) therefore the category "Son of David" is insufficient to account for who the Messiah is.
- [OBS] Matthew 22:46 records: "And no man was able to answer him a word, neither durst any man from that day forth ask him any more questions." The citation produces silence and ends the entire series of temple debates.
- [INTERP] Christ's citation does not explicitly state who he is claiming to be. He poses a problem — "how is he his son?" — without completing the answer in the text. [INTERP] The implied answer is that the Messiah must be something greater than a Davidic descendant in the purely genealogical sense — Lord of David, not merely son of David. The audience is left to draw the conclusion that the Messiah must have a nature or status that transcends ordinary human descent.
- [INTERP] The phrase "David in spirit" — meaning David spoke under divine inspiration — is Christ's own framing of the psalm's authority. He is treating Ps 110 not as human poetry but as a divinely given oracle, which elevates the weight of its testimony. If David spoke by the Spirit, then the one David calls "my Lord" is not a future political king but a figure whose lordship is real from David's present perspective.
- [OBS] This is a self-identification occasion in the sense that Christ is directing attention to the question of his own identity — but he does not say "I am the one David calls Lord." The self-identification is structural and implied, not declarative.

### Occasion 2 — Matt 26:64: Trial Before the High Priest

- Setting and audience: The Sanhedrin court, the night before the crucifixion. Christ has been led to Caiaphas; false witnesses have failed to produce consistent testimony; the high priest asks Christ directly: "I adjure thee by the living God, that thou tell us whether thou be the Christ, the Son of God."
- How Christ introduces the citation: "Jesus saith unto him, Thou hast said: nevertheless I say unto you, Hereafter shall ye see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven" (v. 64).
- Citation type: Composite allusion — Ps 110:1 ("sitting on the right hand of power") combined with Dan 7:13 ("coming in the clouds of heaven"). The two OT texts are woven into a single statement.
- Use: Self-identification — direct, declarative answer to the high priest's question
- [OBS] This is the only occasion in Matthew where Christ answers the question "are you the Christ?" with something approaching a direct affirmative. "Thou hast said" is an affirmation in the idiom of the time.
- [OBS] The response merges two OT texts: Ps 110:1 (sitting at the right hand) and Dan 7:13 (Son of man in the clouds). Together they form a declaration of divine authority and eschatological judgment-role. The high priest does not need to interpret; he recognizes the claim and calls it blasphemy.
- [OBS] The response is not to scribes or Pharisees but to the highest judicial authority in Jerusalem. The audience is entirely different from Occasion 1 — this is not a public teaching moment; it is a private judicial interrogation.
- [INTERP] In Occasion 1, Christ poses the psalm as a puzzle for the Pharisees to work through. In Occasion 2, he applies it to himself directly and declares it will be fulfilled — "hereafter shall ye see." The shift from question to declaration, and from public temple court to private night trial, marks these as genuinely different occasions with different purposes.
- [INTERP] "Sitting on the right hand of power" substitutes "power" for "the LORD" (as in Ps 110:1's "right hand of the LORD"). This may be a circumlocution for the divine name, common in Jewish usage of the period. The substitution does not change the meaning but adapts the idiom for the judicial context.
- [OBS] The high priest's response — "He hath spoken blasphemy" (v. 65) — confirms that the claim was understood as a claim to divine status. The citation of Ps 110:1 in this context is heard as Christ claiming to share the divine throne.

## Across-Occasion Analysis

[OBS] Both citations quote or allude to Ps 110:1, but in very different modes:
- Matt 22: Christ asks a question, citing the psalm as a puzzle. He does not answer the puzzle in public.
- Matt 26: Christ makes a declaration, citing the psalm as a promise. He answers the highest court with a direct claim.

[OBS] The audiences could not be more different: Pharisees in the temple courts versus the high priest at a night trial. One occasion is public; the other is judicial and private.

[OBS] The Matt 26 citation is composite — it merges Ps 110:1 with Dan 7:13, which does not occur in Matt 22. In Matt 22, the psalm stands alone.

[INTERP] The two occasions together form a movement: Christ first raises the identity question publicly (who is David's Lord?), then answers it privately under oath before the court that will condemn him. The public occasion withholds the conclusion; the private occasion declares it. Whether Christ designed this sequence or whether the occasions are simply two independent uses of the same text cannot be determined from the text alone.

[INTERP] In both occasions, the psalm functions as a right-hand-of-God claim. In Matt 22, the claim is embedded in a question about David's son; in Matt 26, the claim is declared as Christ's own future state. Both uses position Christ as the one David called "my Lord" — not merely a son of David but one superior to David.

## What This Choice Reveals About Christ

[INTERP] Ps 110:1 is the only OT text Christ uses to directly address the question of his own identity relative to God. He does not use an Isaiah passage for this purpose, nor a Mosaic text. He uses a Davidic psalm that contains a puzzle about who David would call "Lord" — and he poses that puzzle publicly before answering it under oath.

[INTERP] The choice of this particular verse to raise the identity question in Matt 22 suggests that Christ found the Davidic-descent framework inadequate for describing himself. He did not reject the Son-of-David title (the crowds use it throughout Matthew), but he used Ps 110:1 to establish that it did not capture everything.

[INTERP] The use of Ps 110:1 at the trial — combined with Dan 7:13 — is the closest the Synoptic Gospels come to Christ declaring his divine nature outright. That he chose prophetic scripture to make this declaration, rather than a theological argument, is consistent with his overall pattern of letting the scriptures speak.

## Spiritual Principle

Occasion 1 (Matt 22, temple debate): The question of who the Messiah is cannot be resolved within the category "Son of David" alone. Something more is required — a category that can account for why David would call his own descendant "my Lord."

Occasion 2 (Matt 26, night trial): The position at the right hand of God is not future speculation but a declaration Christ makes about himself under oath, at the moment of greatest human vulnerability, to the court that will condemn him.

Synthesis: Christ uses Ps 110:1 to press in the direction of his own nature — first as a question the audience must wrestle with, then as a declaration they will witness. The spiritual principle is that covenant sonship (Son of David) is real but incomplete; the full identity requires a framework that goes beyond biological descent.

## Interpretive Cautions

- The phrase "Thou hast said" in Matt 26:64 is a recognized affirmative idiom but is not identical to "Yes." Some translations render it differently. Do not claim that Christ gives an unambiguous declarative "yes" without acknowledging the idiom.
- "Sitting on the right hand of power" in Matt 26:64 paraphrases Ps 110:1; it does not quote it exactly. The paraphrase substitutes "power" for "the LORD." Note the variation.
- In Matt 22, Christ poses a puzzle but does not state the answer in the text. Do not claim that he explicitly identifies himself as the one David calls Lord; he implies it without stating it.
- The connection between Ps 110:1 (right hand) and Ps 110:4 (Melchizedek priesthood) is significant in LDS doctrinal context, but Christ does not cite v. 4 in any Gospel passage. Do not import the Melchizedek priesthood connection into an analysis of vv. 41–45 without official Church source support.
