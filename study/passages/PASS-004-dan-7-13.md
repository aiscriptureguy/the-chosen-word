## OT Original Context

[OBS] Daniel 7 records a night vision given to Daniel — the first of four visions in the second half of the book. The vision begins with four great beasts rising from the sea: a lion with eagle's wings (v. 4), a bear with three ribs in its mouth (v. 5), a four-headed leopard (v. 6), and a fourth terrible beast with iron teeth and ten horns (v. 7). The fourth beast is the most fearsome; a small horn arises among the ten horns, with eyes and a mouth speaking great words.

[OBS] Verses 9–10 shift the scene: "I beheld till the thrones were cast down, and the Ancient of days did sit, whose garment was white as snow, and the hair of his head like the pure wool: his throne was like the fiery flame, and his wheels as burning fire. A fiery stream issued and came forth from before him: thousand thousands ministered unto him, and ten thousand times ten thousand stood before him: the judgment was set, and the books were opened."

[OBS] The "Ancient of days" is the designation for the divine figure who presides over this heavenly court. The description — white garments, white hair, fiery throne, ten thousand times ten thousand servants — is a vision of overwhelming divine majesty and judicial authority. The books are opened: this is a scene of cosmic judgment.

[OBS] Verses 11–12: The fourth beast is slain and its body destroyed by fire (v. 11). The other three beasts have their dominion taken away but their lives extended for a season (v. 12).

[OBS] Verse 13 — the cited verse: "I saw in the night visions, and, behold, one like the Son of man came with the clouds of heaven, and came to the Ancient of days, and they brought him near before him."

[OBS] Verse 14: "And there was given him dominion, and glory, and a kingdom, that all people, nations, and languages, should serve him: his dominion is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom that which shall not be destroyed."

[INTERP] In its OT setting, "one like the Son of man" is a figure who approaches the heavenly court riding or surrounded by clouds. The phrase "son of man" in Aramaic (bar enash) means a human being — "one like a human being" or "one having the form of a man." The figure's cloud-riding distinguishes him: in the ancient Near Eastern world, cloud-riding was a characteristic of deity, not humanity. The "son of man" figure in Daniel is human in form but moves in a divine medium.

[INTERP] The movement in vv. 13–14 is not from heaven to earth but from one position before the Ancient of days to a closer position: "they brought him near before him." This is a scene of investiture — the son of man figure receives dominion, glory, and a universal, eternal kingdom from the Ancient of days. He is not descending to judge; he is being granted authority in the heavenly court.

[OBS] The scope of the kingdom given is universal: "all people, nations, and languages." The everlasting nature is stated twice: "his dominion is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom that which shall not be destroyed" (v. 14). This language explicitly contrasts with the beast kingdoms, which are temporary and are destroyed.

[INTERP] First-century Jewish readers interpreted Dan 7:13 messianically — the son of man figure was understood by many as the expected deliverer or king who would receive universal dominion. The phrase "son of man" also became a title Jesus used to refer to himself throughout the Gospels — a usage that is sufficiently distinctive that it stands as one of the most discussed features of Gospel research. [Note: the broader discussion of "son of man" as a title involves scholarly territory beyond approved sources; the above is a general orientation.]

[OBS] The LDS tradition holds that Daniel saw Christ in this vision. The combination of cloud-riding, heavenly court presentation, and receipt of eternal universal dominion fits the Latter-day Saint understanding of Christ's exalted role as King and Judge. [Note: this claim about LDS doctrinal identification requires official Church source verification before being stated as a settled doctrinal conclusion in publishable content. Flagged for Chase's review.]

## LDS Doctrinal Context: The Ancient of Days

[OBS] In LDS scripture, the "Ancient of days" of Daniel 7 is identified as Adam. D&C 138:38 records that in Joseph F. Smith's vision of the spirit world, he saw "Father Adam, the Ancient of Days and father of all" among the great and mighty ones assembled in the vast congregation of the righteous. The identification is explicit and occurs in a vision context — the Ancient of Days is named and then described as the father of all humanity.

[OBS] D&C 116:1 names a specific location: Spring Hill in Daviess County, Missouri, which the Lord designated "Adam-ondi-Ahman, because, said he, it is the place where Adam shall come to visit his people, or the Ancient of Days shall sit, as spoken of by Daniel the prophet." The verse explicitly connects the phrase "the Ancient of Days shall sit" to Daniel's prophecy. The cross-references in the LDS text link D&C 116:1 directly to Daniel 7:13–14, 22.

[OBS] D&C 107:53–57 records a pre-mortal historical event at Adam-ondi-Ahman: three years before his death, Adam gathered Seth, Enos, Cainan, Mahalaleel, Jared, Enoch, and Methuselah — all high priests — together with the righteous residue of his posterity. The Lord appeared to this assembly. Those gathered honored Adam, "calling him Michael, the prince, the archangel" (v. 54). The Lord told Adam: "I have set thee to be at the head; a multitude of nations shall come of thee, and thou art a prince over them forever" (v. 55). Adam then prophesied concerning all his posterity to the latest generation.

[OBS] D&C 116 further implies a future fulfillment of Adam-ondi-Ahman: Adam "shall come to visit his people, or the Ancient of Days shall sit" — the verb tense is future, pointing to a gathering yet to occur. D&C 107:53–57 describes a past gathering; D&C 116:1 anticipates a future one.

[INTERP] In LDS doctrinal understanding, the scene in Daniel 7:9–14 therefore involves at least two identifiable figures: the Ancient of Days (Adam, in his capacity as Michael the archangel and patriarch of the human family) presiding over the heavenly court; and "one like the Son of man" (understood in LDS tradition as Christ) approaching that court to receive universal, everlasting dominion. The heavenly investiture is between the father of the human family and the Redeemer of that family.

[INTERP] This identification does not appear in the Gospels. Neither Matthew 24:30 nor Matthew 26:64 mentions Adam, Michael, or Adam-ondi-Ahman. Any reading that imports the LDS doctrinal content into the Gospel text itself would go beyond what Matthew states. The LDS identification enriches what Daniel 7:9–14 means in the larger restored-gospel framework; it does not change the wording or immediate literary function of Christ's allusion in the NT citations.

## NT Citation Occasions

### Occasion 1 — Matt 24:30: Olivet Discourse

- Setting and audience: The Mount of Olives, private setting. After leaving the temple (24:1), Jesus is alone with his disciples (24:3 specifies "privately" — Peter, James, John, and Andrew ask him). The disciples ask: "Tell us, when shall these things be? and what shall be the sign of thy coming, and of the end of the world?" This is eschatological teaching to the inner circle.
- How Christ introduces the citation: "And then shall appear the sign of the Son of man in heaven: and then shall all the tribes of the earth mourn, and they shall see the Son of man coming in the clouds of heaven with power and great glory" (v. 30).
- Citation type: Close verbal allusion (not a formal quotation formula). The language of "Son of man coming in the clouds of heaven" clearly echoes Dan 7:13, but Christ does not say "as Daniel prophesied" or "it is written." He speaks as the subject of the prophecy.
- Use: Fulfillment claim — Christ describes a future event using Dan 7:13 language, implicitly identifying himself as the "son of man" figure
- [OBS] The Olivet Discourse uses the title "Son of man" throughout (vv. 27, 30, 37, 39, 44). The discourse is entirely eschatological — signs of the coming, tribulation, the gathering of the elect. Verse 30 is the climactic description: after the cosmic disruptions of v. 29 (sun darkened, moon failing, stars falling), the Son of man appears.
- [OBS] The direction of movement in Matt 24:30 differs from Dan 7:13 in one important respect: in Daniel, the son of man moves toward the Ancient of days (from earth-ward to heaven-ward, or within the heavenly court). In Matt 24:30, the Son of man is "coming in the clouds of heaven" — which in context implies coming toward the earth, or appearing to those on earth. The elect are gathered from the earth in v. 31.
- [INTERP] Christ's application of Dan 7:13 language to the Second Coming involves a directional reorientation: what Daniel describes as an approach to the heavenly throne is applied to an appearance to the inhabitants of the earth. Whether this is a reinterpretation of Daniel or a description of a two-stage event (heavenly investiture completed, now coming to earth) is a matter of interpretation. Labeled [INTERP].
- [OBS] The audience in the Olivet Discourse is exclusively the inner circle — the disciples. This is consistent with the Loop 2 pattern finding that Daniel citations appear only in private/inner-circle settings.
- [INTERP] Christ speaks in the first person throughout the Olivet Discourse, using "I" (e.g., "I say unto you"). When he uses "Son of man" he is referring to himself by that title. The allusion to Dan 7:13 is therefore simultaneously self-identification and prophecy — he is identifying himself as the figure Daniel saw and prophesying that what Daniel described will happen.

### Occasion 2 — Matt 26:64: Trial Before the High Priest

- Setting and audience: Sanhedrin court, night before the crucifixion. (See PASS-003 for full context.) The high priest has adjured Christ to declare whether he is the Christ, the Son of God.
- How Christ introduces the citation: "Thou hast said: nevertheless I say unto you, Hereafter shall ye see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven" (v. 64).
- Citation type: Composite allusion — Dan 7:13 ("coming in the clouds of heaven") fused with Ps 110:1 ("sitting on the right hand"). Neither is introduced with a citation formula; both are woven into a single declaration.
- Use: Self-identification — direct declaration to the high priest under adjuration
- [OBS] The phrase "coming in the clouds of heaven" is the Dan 7:13 element. The phrase "sitting on the right hand of power" is the Ps 110:1 element. In Dan 7:13, the son of man approaches the Ancient of days; in Ps 110:1, the Lord invites someone to sit at his right hand. Christ's declaration fuses these two movements into one statement of his own future state.
- [OBS] The high priest hears this as blasphemy (v. 65). The reason is that the combined claim — sitting at God's right hand and coming in divine clouds — would constitute a claim to divine status or at minimum to the highest imaginable heavenly position.
- [INTERP] The Olivet Discourse context (Occasion 1) described what others would see; the trial context describes what the high priest himself will see ("hereafter shall ye see"). The audience of the declaration shifts from disciples who asked for private teaching to the judicial authority who asked for a public declaration of identity.
- [INTERP] In Occasion 1, the Dan 7:13 allusion is eschatological and addressed to believers (inner disciples asking about the end). In Occasion 2, the same allusion is juridical and addressed to the hostile court — it functions as a declaration of authority that will vindicate Christ before the very court that condemns him.
- [OBS] The word "hereafter" in Matt 26:64 indicates future fulfillment: "hereafter shall ye see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power." The high priest will not merely hear the claim; he will eventually see it fulfilled. This transforms the citation into a prophecy directed at the specific people present.

## Across-Occasion Analysis

[OBS] Both citations of Dan 7:13 appear in Matthew. Both use the phrase "Son of man coming in the clouds of heaven" (or close equivalent). Both are addressed to different audiences — the inner disciples (private eschatological instruction) and the highest judicial authority (public declaration under oath).

[OBS] In both occasions, the Dan 7:13 language is used without a citation formula. Christ does not say "as Daniel wrote" or "it is written." He speaks the language as his own.

[INTERP] This is a significant difference from the Hosea 6:6 citations (where Christ says "go ye and learn what that meaneth") and the Lev 19:18 citations (where he introduces the text with "ye have heard" or "it is written"). With Dan 7:13, Christ appropriates the text rather than citing it as an external authority. He speaks as the referent of the prophecy, not as someone teaching from it.

[OBS] In the Olivet Discourse (Occasion 1), Daniel's language describes a future public event — all tribes mourning, all seeing. In the trial (Occasion 2), the same language is directed at specific individuals — "hereafter shall ye see." Occasion 1 is cosmic and universal; Occasion 2 is personal and judicial.

[INTERP] The two occasions together suggest that Dan 7:13 functioned for Christ as language that could carry both eschatological (end-of-age) and immediately judicial meaning. In the discourse, it describes the Second Coming; in the trial, it describes the validation of his claim by those who condemned him. These may not be the same moment — they may be two different fulfillments of the same prophetic image.

[INTERP] When read through the LDS doctrinal identification established in D&C 116:1, 107:53–57, and 138:38, the heavenly court scene in Daniel 7:9–14 takes on a familial and patriarchal dimension: the Ancient of Days (Adam) — father of the entire human family — presides over the investiture of a descendant, the Son of man (Christ). The court that grants everlasting dominion is not an anonymous divine bureaucracy; it is headed by the first man, who was set "at the head" and declared "a prince over them forever" (D&C 107:55). The Son of man approaching "before him" (Dan 7:13) is approaching the patriarch of his mortal lineage.

[INTERP] What this means for Christ's declaration at his trial (Matt 26:64) is worth holding as an open question rather than a settled conclusion: when Christ tells the high priest "hereafter shall ye see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven," the background scene in Daniel involves an orderly transfer of universal authority from the father of humanity to the Redeemer of humanity. Whether Christ invoked that specific dimension of the Danielic image — or whether his audience could have grasped it — is not answerable from the text of Matthew alone and is labeled [INTERP]. It is, however, a dimension of Daniel 7:13 that LDS scripture explicitly endorses, and it adds depth to the passage record.

## What This Choice Reveals About Christ

[INTERP] Christ's use of Dan 7:13 is distinct from every other OT citation in the catalog in one important way: he does not cite it as prophecy for someone else to understand. He identifies himself as the one described. In the Olivet Discourse he describes the Son of man coming as a future fact; at the trial he declares himself to be that Son of man.

[INTERP] The choice of Daniel rather than Isaiah or the Psalms for this particular claim is striking. Daniel 7:13 provides the specific image of coming with clouds — a cloud-riding motif that in the ancient world was associated with divine power. The image is judicial (the heavenly court in Dan 7 is a judgment scene) and eschatological (the kingdom given is everlasting). Both dimensions — judgment and eternal kingdom — appear in both NT occasions.

[INTERP] That Christ reserves Dan 7:13 language for two settings — private eschatological teaching and his own judicial interrogation — suggests the text carried both a pedagogical function (teaching disciples about the end) and a testimonial function (declaring his own identity to authorities). The two functions are not in tension; they are two applications of the same prophetic image.

## Spiritual Principle

Occasion 1 (Matt 24:30, Olivet Discourse): The Second Coming of Christ will be unmistakable — cloud-accompanied, seen by all tribes, preceded by cosmic signs. Believers are not watching for hidden things but for the most public event imaginable.

Occasion 2 (Matt 26:64, night trial): The authority that is denied and condemned in one moment will be publicly vindicated in another. The court that condemns will see the judgment reversed.

Synthesis: Christ uses Dan 7:13 to press toward the reality of ultimate divine authority — his own. The principle in both occasions is that what is contested now (his identity, his authority, his right to make the claims he makes) will be visibly, unmistakably confirmed. The clouds are not decorative; they are a marker of divine presence that Daniel recognized and Christ claims for himself.

## Interpretive Cautions

- The direction of movement in Dan 7:13 (son of man approaches the Ancient of days) is different from Matt 24:30 (coming toward the earth). Do not flatten this difference when presenting the OT context. The connection is real but requires careful description.
- "Son of man" in Daniel is an Aramaic phrase meaning a human figure. That Jesus applies it to himself does not mean Daniel 7:13 predicted the exact title. The appropriation of the image is textual; a full theological account of why Jesus used "Son of man" as a self-designation requires more than can be derived from Dan 7:13 alone.
- The LDS identification of the Ancient of Days with Adam is established in LDS scripture: D&C 138:38 calls Adam "the Ancient of Days and father of all"; D&C 116:1 explicitly connects Spring Hill / Adam-ondi-Ahman to the phrase "the Ancient of Days shall sit, as spoken of by Daniel the prophet." This identification is not flagged or tentative within the LDS doctrinal framework — it is stated in canonized revelation. What remains appropriately cautious is the application of this identification to the Gospel text: neither Matthew 24:30 nor Matthew 26:64 mentions Adam, and Christ's own citation of Dan 7:13 does not explicitly invoke the LDS understanding of who the Ancient of Days is. The doctrinal content of Daniel 7:9–14 in the LDS framework enriches the background of the passage; it does not appear on the surface of the NT citations.
- The composite citation in Matt 26:64 merges Dan 7:13 and Ps 110:1. The two texts address different moments: enthronement (Ps 110:1) and investiture/approach (Dan 7:13). Do not treat the composite as if a single OT scene is being quoted.
