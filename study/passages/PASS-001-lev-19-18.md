## OT Original Context

Leviticus 19 opens with Jehovah commanding Moses to speak to the congregation of Israel: "Ye shall be holy: for I the LORD your God am holy" (v. 2). [OBS] The entire chapter is a catalogue of behavioral commandments — probably fifty or more distinct rules — that together enact what covenant holiness looks like in daily life. They cover: honoring parents; keeping the sabbath; avoiding idols; managing peace offerings correctly; leaving field corners unharvested for the poor and the stranger; prohibitions on theft, lying, and false swearing; protecting the vulnerable (workers, the deaf, the blind); just judgment that does not favor either the rich or the poor; prohibitions on slander and gossip; and a cluster of holiness regulations on mixed breeding, crop mixing, and mixed garments.

[OBS] Verses 17–18 form a unit that moves from the internal condition (not hating a brother in the heart, v. 17) through interpersonal confrontation (rebuke your neighbor, v. 17) to the positive replacement of grudge and vengeance with love (v. 18). The sequence is:

- v. 17: "Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thine heart: thou shalt in any wise rebuke thy neighbour, and not suffer sin upon him."
- v. 18: "Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the Lord."

[OBS] The phrase "children of thy people" in v. 18 specifies the neighbor as the covenant community of Israel. The Septuagint renders this as "the sons of your people," and Jewish interpretation at the time of Jesus debated whether "neighbor" extended beyond this community. The chapter later (vv. 33–34) gives a parallel command about the stranger or foreigner ("love him as thyself"), which suggests the original scope in Leviticus was the covenant community, with the stranger treated as a separate but analogous case.

[OBS] The chapter closes with "I am the LORD" or "I am the LORD your God" repeated throughout as a rationale for each cluster of commandments. This formulaic phrase occurs at least sixteen times in the chapter. [INTERP] This pattern grounds every social command in the character of God, not merely in social utility. Loving the neighbor is not a policy recommendation; it is an expression of covenant identity with a holy God.

[INTERP] Leviticus 19:18 functions in its original context as a summary or culminating command within a section on interpersonal relations (vv. 11–18). It resolves the sequence that began with prohibitions on stealing and lying and ends with the positive principle that replaces vengeance. The verse is a distillation, not an isolated rule.

[OBS] The words "as thyself" set the standard of love at the self-referential level — not a vague encouragement toward goodwill but a comparison to the care and attention one naturally gives to oneself. No definition of "love" is given; the self provides the measure.

## NT/BoM Citation Occasions

### Occasion 1 — Matt 5:43–44: Sermon Antithesis 6

- Setting and audience: Public teaching, hillside in Galilee. The Sermon on the Mount is addressed to disciples (5:1–2) within earshot of the multitudes (7:28). This is Christ's earliest extended public discourse in Matthew. The crowd is not hostile.
- How Christ introduces the citation: "Ye have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy."
- Citation type: Paraphrase with citation formula. "Thou shalt love thy neighbour" is a recognizable summary of Lev 19:18. "And hate thine enemy" is not in Leviticus — it does not appear anywhere in the Mosaic law. [OBS] Christ is citing a form of the commandment that has been augmented by popular tradition or teaching. The Leviticus original says nothing about hating enemies.
- Use: Principle (corrected and extended beyond its received form)
- [OBS] Christ responds with "But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you" (v. 44). He then gives a rationale drawn from the Father's own behavior: God makes his sun rise on the evil and the good alike (v. 45). The standard is divine impartiality, not human reciprocity.
- [OBS] The antithesis structure throughout Matt 5 is "Ye have heard... But I say." This does not signal that Christ is overturning the commandment itself but that he is correcting the received interpretation and pressing toward its deeper intent. In this instance, the augmented form ("hate thine enemy") is what he corrects.
- [INTERP] By stripping away "hate thine enemy" and extending "love thy neighbour" to include enemies, Christ is restoring the Leviticus command to something closer to its original scope (where a parallel command to love the stranger already existed) while also pushing it beyond any boundary the original might have implied.
- [INTERP] The conclusion of this antithesis — "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect" (v. 48) — places the love-neighbor command within the frame of divine imitation. The standard is no longer the covenant community's welfare but God's own character.

### Occasion 2 — 3 Ne 12:43–45: Sermon at Bountiful Antithesis 6

- Setting and audience: Christ speaks to the assembled Nephites after his appearance among them. The audience is described as those who survived the destructions, gathered at the temple. This is not a parallel account of the Galilean sermon but a second, distinct delivery.
- How Christ introduces the citation: "And behold it is written also, that thou shalt love thy neighbor and hate thine enemy."
- Citation type: Paraphrase with citation formula, using "it is written" — a more explicit citation marker than the Galilean "ye have heard."
- Use: Principle (same correction as Occasion 1, addressed to a different population)
- [OBS] The text is nearly parallel to Matt 5:43–48, with some variations. In 3 Ne 12:45, the rain-on-the-just rationale is abbreviated. In 3 Ne 12:46, Christ adds: "Therefore those things which were of old time, which were under the law, in me are all fulfilled." This fulfillment declaration is explicit at Bountiful.
- [OBS] The Bountiful version of v. 48 reads: "Therefore I would that ye should be perfect even as I, or your Father who is in heaven is perfect" — adding "even as I" as a standard alongside the Father.
- [INTERP] The fulfillment declaration in 3 Ne 12:46 ("in me are all fulfilled") changes the interpretive frame around the antithesis. In the Galilean version, Christ is pressing toward a higher law. In the Nephite version, he explicitly says that "old things are done away." The paraphrase of Lev 19:18 is therefore placed within a covenant-transition statement — this is the commandment that is being fulfilled and simultaneously elevated.
- [INTERP] The Nephite audience has different covenant background than the Galilean audience. The explicit fulfillment language at Bountiful may respond to the Nephite tradition of keeping the law of Moses, giving the antithesis a more direct theological function there.

### Occasion 3 — Matt 22:37–39: Greatest Commandment

- Setting and audience: Jerusalem temple courts, during the last week of Christ's ministry. A lawyer (Pharisee) asks "tempting him" — the question is hostile in intent. The audience is the assembled Pharisees.
- How Christ introduces the citation: He first quotes Deut 6:5 ("Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind" — v. 37). Then: "And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (v. 39).
- Citation type: Direct quotation. The Leviticus wording is given precisely: "Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself."
- Use: Proof (demonstrating the internal structure of the entire law)
- [OBS] Christ does not just cite Lev 19:18 in isolation. He pairs it with Deut 6:5 (the Shema) as the second of two commandments. He then says: "On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets" (v. 40). The pairing is the argument, not the individual citations.
- [OBS] The lawyer's question is "which is the great commandment" — singular. Christ answers with two. The move from one to two is exegetically significant. [INTERP] By insisting that love-of-neighbor is "like unto" love-of-God — not subordinate to it but structurally parallel — Christ places interpersonal conduct at the same structural level as the vertical covenant relationship.
- [INTERP] This is the only occasion where Christ explicitly names Lev 19:18 as one of two foundational commandments holding the whole law together. He is not interpreting it as one rule among many but as a structural principle from which the rest of the law derives its meaning.
- [OBS] The response silences the questioner. No objection follows. The Pharisees' hostile intention is thwarted not by refusal to answer but by an answer that reorganizes what they thought they were asking about.

### Occasion 4 — Matt 19:17–19: Rich Young Ruler

- Setting and audience: A private-ish encounter on the road (the setting is transitional — Jesus has just left Galilee and is moving toward Jerusalem). A young man asks "what good thing shall I do, that I may have eternal life?"
- How Christ introduces the citation: After listing several Decalogue commandments (no murder, no adultery, no stealing, no false witness, honor father and mother), Christ adds: "and, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (v. 19).
- Citation type: Direct quotation, appended to the Decalogue list.
- Use: Principle (framing the basic conditions of the life Christ is pointing beyond)
- [OBS] The question asked is about "eternal life," not about the greatest commandment or about enemies. The answer to that particular question is the commandments. [OBS] Lev 19:18 is the only citation in this list that is not from the Decalogue. The Decalogue commandments come from Exodus 20; Lev 19:18 is Christ's addition to that list.
- [OBS] The young man says he has kept all these from his youth. Christ then says: "If thou wilt be perfect, go and sell that thou hast, and give to the poor... and come and follow me" (v. 21). The young man leaves sorrowful.
- [INTERP] The inclusion of Lev 19:18 alongside the Decalogue commandments may be deliberate: the young man's claim to have kept the love-neighbor commandment will be tested immediately by whether he will give to the poor — a specific application of neighbor-love to economic life. Selling possessions to help the poor is precisely what "love thy neighbor as thyself" would require if the neighbor is in need.
- [INTERP] This is not confirmed by the text, which does not spell out the connection. It is an observed structural relationship, not a stated rationale. Marking as [INTERP].
- [OBS] This is the only occasion where Lev 19:18 appears in a conversation about eternal life explicitly. The other three occasions concern either the shape of the law or the scope of the command.

## Across-Occasion Analysis

[OBS] Christ cites Lev 19:18 in four settings: a major public sermon (Galilee), a major public sermon (Bountiful), a hostile doctrinal challenge (Jerusalem temple), and a private seeking conversation (the road). The spread across time, geography, audience, and occasion is wider than for any other single OT passage in the catalog.

[OBS] The citation form varies:
- Matt 5:43 / 3 Ne 12:43: paraphrase with formula, augmented with "hate thine enemy" (which Christ then corrects)
- Matt 22:39: direct quotation, paired with Deut 6:5
- Matt 19:19: direct quotation, appended to Decalogue list

[OBS] The function varies across occasions:
- Sermon uses: corrective extension (expanding beyond the received interpretation)
- Greatest commandment: structural principle (the law hangs on this)
- Rich young ruler: diagnostic criterion (the very standard the young man claims to meet)

[INTERP] In no occasion does Christ present Lev 19:18 as a merely historical or tribal rule. In every use, he treats it as universally binding and foundational to covenant life. The Sermon occasions press it beyond the covenant community. The greatest commandment pairing places it at the structural center of all law. The rich young ruler occasion places it as a personal test.

[INTERP] The pairing of Lev 19:18 with Deut 6:5 in the greatest commandment answer appears to be Christ's own synthesis — there is no OT text that pairs these two as a unit. This pairing is a significant interpretive act, not merely a quotation.

## What This Choice Reveals About Christ

[INTERP] Christ treats Lev 19:18 as if it has never been fully understood. In two of the four occasions, he either corrects its received interpretation (Sermon) or restructures the entire law around it (greatest commandment). He does not cite it as received wisdom to be affirmed but as a principle whose implications have not yet been drawn out.

[INTERP] The four uses together suggest that neighbor-love was not peripheral to Christ's teaching but foundational to the covenant identity he was calling people toward. That this is the only P1 passage in the entire catalog — the only one cited four times in genuinely distinct occasions — marks it as the passage Christ returned to most consistently across every context.

[INTERP] The extension of the command in both Sermon occasions to include enemies suggests that Christ understood the Leviticus command as already reaching beyond tribal boundary but that this implication had been suppressed. Restoring it was part of what he meant by fulfilling the law. This interpretation is tentative and would benefit from verification against official Church sources.

[Note: Any claim about "why Christ chose" this passage as an internal motive is interpretive. The above is labeled [INTERP] accordingly. Official Church commentary would be needed before any of these interpretations could be stated as settled.]

## Spiritual Principle

Occasion 1 (Matt 5 Sermon): The scope of the neighbor-love command reaches past friends and allies to enemies. The standard is divine impartiality — the Father's care for all — not reciprocal human affection.

Occasion 2 (3 Ne 12 Sermon): The same extension is given, but explicitly within the frame of covenant transition: old things are fulfilled in Christ, and the higher law is now the covenant standard.

Occasion 3 (Matt 22 Greatest Commandment): The entire law is organized around two principles — love toward God and love toward neighbor — both equally structural. Nothing in the law stands outside these two.

Occasion 4 (Matt 19 Rich Young Ruler): What you do for your neighbor — concretely, materially — is the test of whether you have actually kept the commandment. Formal compliance is not the same as fulfillment.

Synthesis: Christ uses Lev 19:18 to press in one consistent direction across every audience: love of neighbor is not a secondary or derivative commandment but a first-order covenant obligation, inseparable from love of God, reaching beyond the expected circle, and verified by concrete action rather than formal observance.

## Interpretive Cautions

- The phrase "hate thine enemy" in Matt 5:43 is not from Leviticus. We cannot be certain whether Christ is citing a specific rabbinic teaching, a popular paraphrase, or something from a source not preserved. Avoid stating that "the Pharisees added hate your enemy to the law" without a specific verified source.
- "Neighbor" in Lev 19:18 originally referred to "the children of thy people" (the covenant community). Christ's expansion to enemies is an interpretive move. Do not flatten the OT meaning to make the original match the NT extension.
- The pairing of Deut 6:5 with Lev 19:18 may have precedent in Jewish teaching of the period, or it may be original to Christ. This question cannot be resolved from approved sources alone without expanding the source set.
- The structural connection between Lev 19:18 and the invitation to sell possessions in Matt 19 is an observed possibility, not stated in the text. It should be presented as a possible reading.

The antitheses establish that believers are now under a higher covenant standard, not two parallel systems. The Galilean version implies the same transition even without the explicit fulfillment declaration. This interpretation should be used when writing the site's Occasion 2 summary for 3 Ne 12:43.

3. Christ pairs Deut 6:5 and Lev 19:18 as a unit, with no OT precedent for that pairing. Does that pairing feel like a revelation of something already implicit in the law, or like a new interpretive act?
